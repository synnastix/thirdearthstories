*Extract from Qazari texts relating to trade and dealings with outsiders. This specific text is related to trade relations with the Empire, written in the early 700s CR.*

## The Economy of Empires

The Empire of the Heartlands is a living thing. It breathes. It moves. And what flows through its veins can be measured in blood, food, or coin. I hope this work will help others navigate their systems as it can be quite confusing to those encountering it for the first time.

## Villages

Those expecting a traditional barter system, where one takes what they have to another to execute a direct trade, shall find themselves standing next to a loaded cart for longer than they’d like. To put it simply: 

*That is not how things work.* 

Villages are generally tightly woven, where people live and work as neighbors. David gives grain to Berand after the harvest, and everyone remembers the gesture. The next spring, Berand gives David a lamb. There is no record of what happened – it simply lives in the people who know them both and remember how they treated one another. This is the first rule those new to the Heartlands must learn:

*The people are the ledger.*

With this decentralized accounting system, interdependence grows and shady dealings lessen, at least among those who wish to remain in community with one another. The village runs on remembered favors; the city needs tokens. The boundary between them is where things get interesting.

## The City and Coinage

Coin settles obligations between strangers, bridging the chasm where trust and memory cannot find footing. Within the Heartlands, a very specific hierarchy has emerged, with Empire-controlled ceramic production at the base.

### Ceramic Pieces

Some might expect copper pieces as the lower tier, but within the Heartlands copper is far too precious in other forms. Formed into a cooking pot. Cast into a bronze knife. Indeed, any attempt at using copper as a coin would only result in said coins being melted and reforged into something useful. Instead, the Empire has commissioned the production of ceramic pieces glazed with a special blend of material found close to Timbur Deep and nowhere else. 

The result is a token with an Imperial seal, glossy and near-black with a green break where it thins over the edges. It is both beautiful and instantly recognizable, especially in direct sunlight where specks of something reflective, obsidian perhaps, glints in the light. The glaze mixture itself is tightly controlled through contracted Merchant Houses.

Said Merchant Houses also control the firing, which is done at special kilns designed for the purpose and fueled by the cheapest of fuels. *(This has led to Imperials giving the coin colorful nicknames.)* Further, the coin is scored on the bottom and designed to be easily broken into smaller fragments which they call *shards*, with each shard carrying exactly one-eighth the value of the whole. Cleverly, this serves two functions. First, it allows for a smaller unit to be carried as currency within the Empire. Second, it allows damaged ceramic pieces to retain some value as any damaged pieces can simply be broken off and discarded. 

Counterfeiting of ceramic pieces does not appear to be of significant concern to Imperial authorities and for fair reason. The raw clay material itself is nearly worthless, the final product has little intrinsic value, and it takes significant resources to establish and operate the kiln to fire the coin. Anyone with said resources is likely to be far more profitable crafting and firing other products. We can describe ceramic pieces as only having value because the Empire says they do and this is reflected in trade – beyond the Empire’s borders, they are viewed as shiny bits of pottery and nothing more. 

*A ceramic piece is not wealth, it is a loyalty token that keeps people tied to the Empire.*

### Silver and Gold 

Ceramic pieces may be fine for common use within the Empire, but only at the lowest levels. Silver and gold function as the accounting layer above, settling debts for Domains, Merchant Houses, Legion contracts, and other needs where a cart of ceramic pieces become incredibly impractical.

A quick note about the Empire’s Domains: They sometimes mint their own coin, or at least smelt their own silver and gold bars to use for trade. What they do not do, as far as we know, is fire their own ceramic pieces. Silver and gold carry value at a base level regardless of what image is stamped into it, while the ceramic does not. Thus, if you ever encounter ceramic pieces with a Domain Lord’s emblem, treat it with at least some skepticism.

The metals, and by extension the coins, are valued by weight rather than count. Where they are minted into coins, the metal used weighs roughly one-eighth of an ounce for each coin. The Empire values silver ten times as much as their ceramic pieces. Gold is rarer still, mainly coming into the Empire as an export from other regions. It takes twelve silver pieces to purchase one gold coin, and as such it is a rare thing that many will never see.

Currency minted in other regions may also be accepted within the Heartlands as long as the weight is the same. In some cases the individual Domain Lords tried to tamper with these rates, often trying to increase the value of their own holdings. A Lord holding a silver mine may try to claim silver to be more valuable, for example. These efforts usually fail, and, occasionally, the Empire determines the Lord is no longer acting in the best interest of the Empire and act accordingly.

I am including a simple breakdown here for ease of reference:

**The ladder**

- 8 shards = 1 ceramic piece  
- 10 ceramic pieces = 1 silver piece  
- 12 silver pieces = 1 gold piece  
- 1 gold piece = 960 shards  


### Relative Bargaining Power

The Empire’s system creates a tiered structure which covers most of what the population will encounter. We must acknowledge that exchange rates are far easier to regulate than market values as the price of goods will vary depending on the location and the circumstances of that transaction. It may be helpful to include rough estimates of what certain goods might cost so that one does not walk into the Heartlands only to be fleeced by the locals.

It is worth noting that it is common practice in the Heartlands to pay workers in food or other goods rather than strictly through coin. This is important for traders - in times of famine, the entire economy of the Heartlands becomes less profitable and attention may be best directed elsewhere.

## Gems and Contracts

There are, of course, times when stacks of coins or bars of metals become impractical as well. Domain Lords pay taxes and tributes. Merchant Houses negotiate large deals with one another across long distances. Sometimes trades are worked months or even years in advance. All of these require mechanisms that differ greatly from simple transactions.

### Gems

While some gems may be sought after by alchemists or mages, most gems are ornamental and have no utilitarian value. Wealthy individuals are attracted to gems when they are cut and polished correctly, with larger pieces commanding a higher price in the various markets. The Heartlands is no different in that regard, and there are reports of jewelry worn by nobles, Domain Lords, or Merchant House benefactors whose value rivals that of whole shipments of goods. The stones themselves become a means of storing value where even gold becomes too cumbersome.

What is important to the merchant is that the value of such stones varies tremendously throughout the region. One might be worth a small fortune in Merrow’s Reach while in Timbur Deep it barely registers as something important. Learning the value of each type of gem across the region is as much art as it is science, and warrants its own discussion elsewhere.

### Contracts

Merchant Houses could not function without contracts. It really is that simple.

Yet contracts – which may be called writs, charters, promissory notes, and similar terms – are often used throughout the region when deals either too large or too long-tenured for traditional coin must be executed. A Merchant House may contract with a farmer for the next year’s crop yield, while another may contract for protection services while transporting goods through a region. These contracts may also be traded to another organization or individual willing to agree to the terms, and some organizations even use them as a form of gambling against the future. 

In rare cases the contract may be years-long. These are more common between Merchant Houses where two anticipate needing the services of another for some time. For example, one may operate a mine, and another operates a smelter. In that case, a contract will help reduce tension in disagreements that are short-lived. The paper itself is never worth anything, but the infrastructure behind it is worth everything.

Merchant Houses generally use the torn paper method to prevent forgeries. This method involves tearing a sheet of paper down the center, then assembling the two halves to form a complete sheet. Some houses prefer to align the straight edges of each half to make it easier to write across, while others prefer to reassemble the sheet at the tear. In either case, distinct markings are written across the seam and both parties apply a signature or seal which spans both halves.
